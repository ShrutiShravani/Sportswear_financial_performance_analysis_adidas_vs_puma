Final Report: European Sportswear Financial Performance Analysis — adidas vs Puma (2022–2025)
1. Revenue and Profitability
adidas

2022 → 2023 (revenue -4.8%, gross margin 47.3% → 47.5%): adidas ended its Yeezy partnership after Kanye West's antisemitic comments, which dragged revenue down by around €500m in the comparison, and the company deliberately reduced wholesale sell-in to cut high inventory levels. Gross margin still improved slightly for the full year despite this, though it fell sharply in some quarters (44.8% in Q1) on higher supply chain costs and discounting, before recovering later in the year. CEO Bjørn Gulden said the company "reduced our inventories by almost €1.5 billion" through disciplined buying. Sources: CNBC, SportBusiness.

2023 → 2024 (revenue +10.5%, gross margin 47.5% → 50.8%): Full recovery year, with double-digit brand growth across all markets and the Yeezy drag no longer present. Source: alchempro.com.

2024 → 2025 (revenue +4.8%, gross margin 50.8% → 51.6%): Currency-neutral growth of 13%, driven by broad market and channel growth, gross margin gain from lower product/freight costs and reduced discounting, despite over €1bn of currency headwinds. Sources: adidas press release, retailsector.co.uk.

Puma

2022 (record year, for context): revenue grew 24.4%, on broad-based demand across regions. Source: fashionunited.uk.

2022 → 2023 (revenue +1.6%): growth slowed sharply from 2022's 24%, in a year Puma itself flagged as highly uncertain (Ukraine war, inflation, freight cost pressure). Not fully sourced for the specific full-year drivers.

2023 → 2024 (revenue +2.5%, corrected): Our initial pull from yfinance showed -2.4% for this period, conflicting with Puma's own reporting. Puma's audited 2024 annual report confirms actual 2024 sales were €8,817.2m, not the €8,398.0m yfinance returned — a source data error, now corrected via a manual override (see clean_financials.py). The real figure is +2.5% reported growth (+4.4% currency-neutral), with a 110 basis point gross margin improvement to 47.4%, though net income fell due to higher net interest expense and higher non-controlling interests. Source: PUMA press release.

2024 → 2025 (revenue -13.1%, gross margin 47.6% → 44.9%): the strategic reset year — deliberate wholesale cuts, inventory clean-up, reduced promotions, worsened by currency (Argentine Peso, US Dollar, Turkish Lira). Sources: PUMA FY2025 release, footwearmagazine.com.

3. Capital Efficiency (ROCE)
adidas

ROCE rose from 6.6% (2022) to 18.5% (2025), with a dip to 2.8% in 2023.

2022→2023 (6.6%→2.8%): EBIT margin collapsed to 1.3% (Yeezy exit, higher discounting). Sources: CNBC, SportBusiness.
2023→2024 (2.8%→11.7%): EBIT margin recovered to 5.5% (Yeezy drag gone, double-digit brand growth). Source: alchempro.com.
2024→2025 (11.7%→18.5%): EBIT margin rose to 8.3% (pricing discipline, lower costs). Capital employed also grew (17% inventory build for the 2026 World Cup), which would normally lower ROCE, but the margin gain outweighed it. Sources: adidas press release, retailsector.co.uk, adidas Annual Report 2025.
Puma

ROCE fell every year, from 16.3% (2022) to −11.0% (2025).

2022→2023 (16.3%→15.1%) and 2023→2024 (15.1%→12.8%): EBIT margin eroded gradually (7.6%→7.2%→6.5%); visible in the numbers but not explained in detail in the sources reviewed.
2024→2025 (12.8%→−11.0%): EBIT margin turned negative (−4.9%) from the strategic reset. Capital employed shrank (assets fell), which would normally support ROCE, but the margin collapse overwhelmed that effect. Sources: PUMA FY2025 release, footwearmagazine.com, PUMA Annual Report 2025.

Summary: ROCE moved almost entirely on margin, not capital efficiency, for both companies. Limitation: Puma's 2022–2024 margin decline is visible but not explained in detail in the sources reviewed.

4. Profit vs. Cash
adidas
2022 (NI €612m, OCF −€479m): Profit reported, but cash burned. Driver not visible in this data (no prior year to compare); unsourced — flagged as an open question.
2023 (NI −€75m, OCF +€2,550m): Loss reported, but strong cash generation — inventory cut (releasing €1,448m) and lower receivables (releasing €623m) outweighed payables paydown (using €632m). Shows the company prioritizing cash health during a loss year.
2024 (NI €764m, OCF +€2,910m): Both strong; payables rose (€820m, releasing cash) more than inventory/receivables build used.
2025 (NI €1,340m, OCF €751m): Profit grew, cash generation weakened — inventory (+€843m), receivables (+€222m), and payables paydown (−€186m) all used cash, entirely explained by the planned World Cup 2026 stock-up already sourced.
Puma
2022 (NI €354m, OCF €418m): Broadly aligned.
2023 (NI €305m, OCF €654m): Cash outperformed profit, aided by inventory falling (releasing €440m).
2024 (NI €282m, OCF €695m): Payables rising (€394m, releasing cash) outweighed a smaller working capital build.
2025 (NI −€646m, OCF −€319m): Loss was worse than the cash outcome — receivables fell sharply (releasing €333m, tied to the Q4 sales collapse), softening the impact of paying down payables (−€622m).

Summary: adidas invested cash into growth in 2025 after prioritizing cash preservation in the 2023 loss year. Puma's 2025 cash position, while negative, was cushioned by the mechanical shrinkage of receivables and payables alongside a smaller revenue base. Limitation: adidas's 2022 cash shortfall despite reported profit is not explained by this data and hasn't been separately sourced.

5. Leverage & Financial Risk
adidas

Debt-to-equity improved steadily (1.29→0.96), interest coverage strengthened (5.3x→9.0x), with a dip to 1.7x in 2023.

2022→2023: Debt fell €896m, equity fell €411m. Interest coverage collapse to 1.7x was driven by the EBIT margin crash, not debt levels.
2023→2024: Equity rose €896m (retained profit), debt flat. Interest coverage recovered with EBIT margin.
2024→2025: Equity rose €300m, debt fell €31m — continued deleveraging. adidas cited its "healthy balance sheet and strong cash flow generation" as the basis for a new €1bn share buyback (funded from 2026 cash flow). Source: adidas press release.
Puma

Debt-to-equity stable through 2024 (0.63→0.71→0.61), then jumped to 1.47 in 2025; interest coverage collapsed from 4.7x to −3.2x.

2024→2025: Equity fell €1,067m (37.7%) — the net loss plus €311.1m of negative currency translation and hedging losses recorded directly in equity. Source: PUMA Annual Report 2025. Debt rose €864m as Puma took on new financing to fund the turnaround — promissory notes totaling €275m and a €500m bridge loan in December 2025, plus heavier revolving credit use. Net debt jumped to €1.064bn from €119.8m a year earlier. Sources: gdnonline.com; PUMA Q4 2025 aide memoire ("leverage is elevated and will stay elevated... throughout the first half of 2026," no covenants breached). Interest coverage went negative simply because operating income itself went negative.

Summary: adidas deleveraged steadily, ending 2025 strong enough to launch a buyback. Puma's balance sheet risk rose sharply in 2025 — losing equity from the loss while adding debt to fund the turnaround — though the company states it has sufficient credit lines and no breached covenants.


5. Budget vs Actual

adidas: naive budget €24,360m, actual €24,811m, variance +€451m (+1.9%) — adidas beat a simple trend-based expectation. A naive model, unaware of any specific management actions, would have predicted continued moderate growth (based on 2023-24's recovery trend) — and reality came in slightly ahead of even that. This is a small, believable beat, not a shock.

Puma: naive budget €8,999m, actual €7,296m, variance -€1,703m (-18.9%) — Puma missed a simple trend-based expectation by a wide margin. A naive model, extrapolating from 2023-24's roughly flat/slightly positive growth, would have predicted revenue near €9 billion. Actual revenue came in almost 19% below that.

adidas: the small positive variance (+1.9%) shows continuity — a trend-following model would have gotten reasonably close to reality, because adidas's 2025 performance was an extension of a recovery already underway in 2023-24, not a sudden new development. The specific drivers you already sourced (pricing discipline, lower freight/product costs) pushed results slightly above trend, but didn't represent a break from it.

Puma: the large negative variance (-18.9%) shows the opposite — no reasonable trend-based model could have predicted this outcome, because the cause wasn't organic business drift, it was a deliberate strategic decision (the wholesale reset, distribution clean-up, reduced promotions) made partway through the year. This is the genuinely senior-level point: a naive budget model works when a business changes gradually, but breaks down completely when management makes a discrete strategic choice. An analyst's job in this situation isn't to trust the model — it's to recognize when qualitative information (a strategy shift) should override a purely quantitative trend.
